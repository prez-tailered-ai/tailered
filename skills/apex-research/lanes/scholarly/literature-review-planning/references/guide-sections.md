# Guide sections

Write as a knowledgeable colleague briefing a newcomer: finite, ordered, honest about gaps. Not exhaustive, not submission-ready.

1. **Topic overview.** One paragraph of four to six sentences: what the field is, why it matters, the framework used, the shape of the evidence, one honest limitation.
2. **Start here.** Five to seven papers in reading order: the best recent review, one or two foundational papers (repeat hits, citations per year), two or three frontier papers, one paper on a live dispute. Each: linked title, authors and year, one sentence on its contribution, one on what to look for.
3. **How the field got here.** A short chronological narrative, a table of five to eight milestones (year, milestone, significance) built from the date-limited searches, and a note on how the terms changed.
4. **Sub-area guides**, one per sub-area:
   - what the research shows, two or three sentences with inline citations;
   - three to five key papers with year, citation count and why each matters;
   - six to ten search terms, including older synonyms and controlled-vocabulary headings where they exist;
   - two or three Boolean strings ready to paste into any index.
5. **Key research groups.** Three to five, from the recurring-author list: lead names, affiliation, sub-areas covered, one representative linked paper, why they matter.
6. **Open questions and gaps** in three groups: methods, populations or contexts, concepts or theory. Each gap has one sentence saying why filling it matters.
7. **Bibliography.** Alphabetical by first author, full link never truncated. Every inline citation appears here; every entry is cited. No orphans either way.
8. **Audit log.** Table of searches (number, query, filters, results, status: ok, zero matches, thin, rate limited, failed), the three counts, the lane used, thin sub-areas, suggested manual follow-up and terminology shifts seen. A reader should be able to rerun it.
