# Sizing methods

## Three figures

- Total: revenue if every buyer in the category bought.
- Serviceable: the part of that you can serve with your geography, segment focus and product.
- Obtainable: the share of serviceable you can realistically win in the planning window, limited by capacity and competition.

## Two directions

- From the top: published category total x serviceable fraction x reachable share. Quick, but inherits the source's bias.
- From the bottom: number of potential customers x annual price x serviceable fraction x adoption rate. Slower, defensible because each unit is named.
- Gap = |top - bottom| / max(top, bottom). Over the decision's tolerance means triangulation failed.

Fermi habit: break the unknown into factors you can estimate, state each assumption, carry the range through.

Fallacies: double counting overlapping segments or value-chain layers; "just 1 percent of a huge number"; treating category growth as your growth; precision the inputs cannot support.

## Survey sample

- Base: n0 = z^2 x p x (1 - p) / e^2, with z = 1.645 (90 percent), 1.96 (95 percent) or 2.576 (99 percent), p the expected proportion (0.5 if unknown), e the margin of error.
- Finite population of size N: n = n0 / (1 + (n0 - 1) / N).
- Each reported segment needs its own n at its own margin; the survey total is at least the sum of segment floors. Example: plus or minus 8 percent at 95 percent with p = 0.5 gives about 150 per segment.
- Beyond sampling error: coverage (the frame misses people), non-response (responders differ), measurement (wording, order, scale). A tight margin on a biased frame is precise and wrong.
- Wording: no leading questions, no two questions in one, watch scale and order effects, pre-test with a few cognitive interviews. Follow recognised disclosure and response-rate standards.

## Segment criteria

| Criterion | Question | Gate |
|---|---|---|
| Measurable | can it be sized and identified? | no |
| Substantial | large and profitable enough to serve? | yes |
| Accessible | reachable through channels you can afford? | yes |
| Differentiable | does it respond differently from other segments? | no |
| Actionable | can you design and run a program for it? | no |

Verdict: target (passes gates, strong on the rest), watch (passes gates, weak elsewhere), drop (fails a gate). Firm or demographic bases are easy to measure but predict behaviour weakly; needs-based (the job the buyer is trying to get done) predicts better. Pair the two. For attribute trade-offs, choice-based methods such as conjoint or best-worst scaling beat asking "how important is X?".

Competitive context comes only from public, ethically obtained sources: no misrepresentation, no confidential information.

## Brief layout

1. Objective: question, decision and decider, precision.
2. Sizing: method, top-down inputs with sources, bottom-up inputs with sources, both results, gap, verdict.
3. Survey plan: population and frame, confidence, margin, expected proportion, segments and per-segment margin, overall and per-segment n, mode and coverage risk.
4. Segments: scores, gates, verdicts.
5. Competitive context: sources, structural forces summary.
6. Assumptions register: every assumption, each traced to a source or labelled unverified.
7. Confidence: high, moderate or low, and why.
